# SAME SEED — Story Bible

## Premise

In 2022, **Evan Mercer**, 43, lives outside Dayton, Ohio. He works desktop support for a regional insurance company, is married to **Claire**, and has a teenage daughter, **Mara**. Evan is clever in an undisciplined way. He reads too much, finishes too little, and becomes obsessed with generative AI mostly because it lets him make things he could never make alone.

Over the next four decades, artificial systems become steadily more capable while Evan's life follows the old human trajectory: love changes shape, a marriage ends, parents die, friends get sick, children leave, technology stops feeling miraculous, and the body becomes less negotiable.

Evan never becomes an AI researcher. He remains a hobbyist, a user, a man at the edge of the room. That is essential. He is not special because he understands the future. He is interesting because he is ordinary enough to experience the future as weather.

As models become less verbal internally, brain-computer interfaces become ordinary, and neuroscience begins describing memory and perception in ways that rhyme disturbingly with machine learning, Evan develops a private suspicion: consciousness is not sealed off from the rest of physics. The brain may be an interface inside a larger computation rather than an island looking out at a dead universe.

The question that finally disturbs him is not “Are we in a simulation?” It is: **Why would the first beings capable of constructing convincing worlds happen to be born at the first moment such worlds become possible?**

He dies without proving anything.

He wakes elsewhere.

## The dramatic promise

For roughly the first half, the film should be playable as a straight domestic drama with unusually prescient technology in the background. No ominous score when AI appears. No glitching birds. No coded messages. No simulation breadcrumbs that require supernatural causes.

Every “hint” has an ordinary explanation until the final act.

The movie earns its speculative turn by making the audience do the same accumulation Evan does.

## Main characters

### EVAN MERCER

43 in 2022. Midwestern, dry humor, restless curiosity, inconsistent follow-through. He can understand the shape of an idea before he can understand the mathematics. He keeps notebooks full of diagrams that begin confidently and trail off into arrows.

His strength is pattern recognition. His flaw is that pattern recognition can become meaning hunger.

He is not depressed by technology. He is thrilled by it. His existential fear comes from the contrast: the machines improve, while everyone he loves remains mortal.

His emotional arc is from **control through explanation** to **acceptance without proof**.

### CLAIRE MERCER

42 in 2022. Occupational therapist. Warm, observant, practical, funny in a sharper way than Evan. She is not “the wife who doesn't understand him.” She understands him very well, including the exhausting part: Evan can turn fear into theories because theories are easier than grief.

Their marriage ends because their needs diverge over years, not because AI steals him away. Their later relationship is gentler than their marriage became.

### MARA MERCER

15 in 2022. Their daughter. She grows into a public-school art teacher and later a mother. As a teenager she treats generative media as completely normal, which unnerves Evan more than the technology itself.

Mara is the story's strongest counterweight to simulation obsession. Her answer to metaphysics is usually some version of: “Maybe. Dinner is still at six.”

She eventually gives Evan the sentence that allows him to stop searching: **“You keep asking what kind of world this is. It is also the world where I am.”**

### RAY MERCER

Evan's father. Retired tool-and-die machinist. He distrusts hype but loves mechanisms. He dies before the technology becomes truly strange. His death is Evan's first encounter with the fact that perfect digital reconstruction would still not answer whether the person is present.

Ray's recurring phrase when repairing anything: **“Find what carries the load.”**

The phrase migrates from mechanics to networks to memory to metaphysics.

### JULIAN PARK

Evan's closest adult friend, met through work. Network engineer, competent where Evan is improvisational. Julian is Korean-American, divorced before the film begins, and approaches AI with amused skepticism.

He becomes the one person who lets Evan talk through increasingly strange ideas without either indulging or humiliating him.

Julian later receives an early neural prosthesis after a stroke, giving the film its most intimate bridge between computation and selfhood.

### DR. LEENA SHAH

Neuroscientist and science communicator whom Evan first encounters through lectures, then briefly meets at a public event years later. She is not a guru and never validates his simulation theory. Her role is to make the brain more material, not more mystical.

Her key idea: **“The brain isn't reading the universe. It is a piece of the universe changing state.”**

Evan carries the sentence much farther than she intends.

## The outer-world characters

After death, identities become difficult to name. The beings who wake are not glowing souls. They are recognizably themselves but also more extensive than the lives they just lived.

### INA

The woman who recognizes Evan first. Familiar before he can place her. She has lived many lives and treats them with the fond exhaustion of someone discussing difficult travel.

She once chose early Imperial Rome because she expected architecture and philosophy. She mostly remembers gastrointestinal disease, smoke, childbirth deaths, and never being alone.

### TOMAS

A man who tried a late-Paleolithic hunter-gatherer life and is mildly irritated by how romanticized it later became.

### THE ATTENDANT

Not a bureaucratic angel. More like a patient host. May itself be an interface. Never explains cosmology. The outer world does not dump lore because everyone there already knows it once the life-state releases.

## Rules of the speculative layer

1. The film never proves that the inner world is literally a computer simulation in the contemporary sense.
2. “Simulation” is Evan's primitive metaphor for a nested level of reality.
3. The outer world is not morally superior. It is materially safer and existentially thinner.
4. Inner lives are not punishments. They are chosen constraints.
5. Memory of the outer level is suppressed during embodiment because certainty would destroy the experience.
6. Some intuition leaks through, but never as actionable supernatural knowledge.
7. AI does not cause awakening. AI merely gives an inner civilization enough conceptual tools to notice the architecture from inside.
8. Death ends the constraint set. It does not erase the lived person.
9. The identities in different lives overlap without being one-to-one costumes. “Avatar” is close but incomplete.
10. The ending should make the audience wonder whether forgetting is a defect or the central feature.

## The AI progression

Technology must advance mostly through casual use, not exposition.

### 2022–2024 — tools
Text generation is funny and unreliable. Image generation is visibly synthetic. Coding assistants feel like autocomplete with ambition. Evan makes little games, visualizers, and useless utilities.

### 2025–2030 — collaborators
Models become persistent, multimodal and competent. Video generation becomes mundane. Games synthesize detail on demand. Personal agents remember years of context. People stop saying “AI-generated” because the distinction has little practical value.

### 2030s — opaque cognition
Systems become capable of long internal computation that no longer maps naturally to language. Their explanations are translations after the fact. “Neuralese” becomes a popular but imprecise term.

### late 2030s–2040s — neural interfaces
Medical BCIs restore movement, language and portions of damaged memory access. Consumer interfaces follow slowly. The cultural shock is not telepathy; it is how quickly people normalize external cognitive machinery.

### later decades — disappearance into infrastructure
AI stops being a subject. It becomes roads, electricity, search, medicine, fabrication, governance support, entertainment, companionship, education. Evan's obsession becomes old-fashioned precisely as the implications become largest.

## Neuroscience progression

The film uses three conceptual bridges:

### 1. Memory is physical change
Evan learns that memories are not files stored in one location. They are changes in distributed biological machinery — engrams, altered synapses, recurrent dynamics, reactivation pathways.

### 2. Perception is active reconstruction
The brain does not passively record the world. It samples, predicts, fills gaps, directs attention and updates itself through error.

### 3. The observer is inside the mechanism
There is no homunculus watching the brain's internal movie. The “watcher” is itself produced by ongoing physical computation.

Evan's leap is philosophical: if the observer is already inside one level of machinery, why assume that machinery is ontologically sealed from larger levels?

The film treats this as an intuition, not a scientific result.

## Recurring motifs

### RESOLUTION
Old game graphics. Upscaled home video. MRI images. Pixelated faces. Reconstructed memories. The outer world eventually appears almost offensively plain and high-resolution.

### COMPRESSION
ZIP files, model weights, old photographs, family stories, a dead person's belongings reduced to boxes. What remains when detail is removed?

### CARRIERS
Ray's phrase: “Find what carries the load.” Network cables. Axons. Marriage. Memory. Bodies. A hand holding another hand.

### ROOMS
Bedroom office. Hospital rooms. Lawyer office. Hotel conference room. Waiting room. The outer world. The final hospital room in the 1970s.

### THE RED BALL
In the opening, toddler-era home video of Mara throwing a red rubber ball toward camera. Evan later uses the clip to test video upscaling. Years later an AI reconstructs frames never recorded. Near death, he remembers the ball crossing sunlight. In the outer world, a red ball rolls through frame with no explanation.

It is not a clue from outside. It is thematic rhyme.

### STATIC / SILENCE
Early models chatter constantly. Later models become quieter. Julian after stroke struggles for words. The most intelligent systems eventually answer with actions rather than explanations. Evan's death is nearly silent.

## What the film is actually about

The movie is not primarily about whether reality is simulated.

It is about the suspicion that **meaning may require constraints**.

A frictionless world can provide pleasure but not stakes. An immortal being can possess endless information but cannot experience irreversible choice unless it temporarily gives up certainty and safety.

The mortal world is therefore not valuable despite pain. The outer beings return because boundedness creates kinds of value unavailable to them otherwise: courage, urgency, forgiveness, risk, discovery, parenthood, grief, surprise.

This should never become a justification for suffering. The film must preserve the moral horror of needless pain. The outer-world characters are not smug about it. Some lives are regretted. Some are abandoned early. Some choices look foolish in retrospect.

The point is narrower: **a life can be real in consequence even if its frame is chosen.**

## Ending logic

After waking, Evan gradually remembers that he has done this before. The “boring utopia” is beautiful, kind and safe — and within minutes of screen time the audience understands why eternity there becomes textureless.

No one forces him back.

He spends what may be years in the outer world. We see projects begun and completed instantly, landscapes altered without cost, relationships without fear of loss, arguments that cannot wound, games whose stakes are reversible. Comfort becomes weightless.

Eventually Evan enters the same quiet room where lives are configured.

He chooses broadly the same world, the same era, perhaps even many of the same relationships. Then he changes one parameter:

> Same world as last time. But AI does not happen.

The Attendant asks whether he wants to remember making the choice.

Evan smiles because of course he knows the answer.

Cut to a 1970s delivery room.

The newborn cries.

The audience understands that removing AI may remove the breadcrumb trail that helped him guess the truth — which means this next life may feel even more completely real.

That is why he chose it.
